Federal job postings list a "grade" instead of a salary range the way private employers do — GS-9, GS-12, GS-13, and so on. Here's how to translate that into an actual number.

## What GS means

GS stands for General Schedule, the pay system that covers most federal civilian white-collar jobs. It runs from GS-1 (entry-level, minimal qualifications) to GS-15 (senior expert / supervisory), with Senior Executive Service (SES) positions above that band entirely.

Each grade typically corresponds to a level of responsibility and required qualifications:

- **GS-5 to GS-7** — entry-level professional positions, often requiring a bachelor's degree or equivalent experience
- **GS-9 to GS-11** — early-career professional roles, sometimes requiring a master's degree or specialized experience
- **GS-12 to GS-13** — mid-career, often the working-level grade for specialists and analysts
- **GS-14 to GS-15** — senior-level, supervisory, or highly specialized technical roles

## Steps within a grade

Each GS grade has 10 "steps," and your pay increases as you move through them — roughly 3% per step, typically on a schedule tied to time in the position (faster in early steps, slower later). A new hire usually starts at Step 1 unless prior federal service or superior qualifications justify a higher step.

## Locality pay: the part people forget

The base GS pay table is not what most federal employees actually earn. On top of it, the government adds "locality pay" — an adjustment based on where the job is physically located, meant to account for regional cost-of-living differences. Locality pay can add well over 20% to base pay in high-cost metro areas, and less in lower-cost regions.

This is where remote positions get interesting: **remote federal employees are generally paid based on their actual residence's locality**, not the hiring agency's headquarters. Read the announcement's pay section carefully — some remote postings specify which locality table applies, and it directly affects your final number.

## How to estimate what a listed GS grade pays

1. Note the grade (e.g., GS-12) and step range listed in the announcement.
2. Look up the current-year base GS pay table (search "OPM GS pay table" for the official, current figures — we don't reproduce it here since it changes annually and we'd rather you use the authoritative source than a number that's gone stale on this page).
3. Apply the locality pay percentage for your actual work location, or the location specified in the announcement for remote roles.
4. The result is your realistic base salary range for that position.

Many announcements on this site already list the specific salary range calculated for that posting — when available, trust that number over a manual calculation, since it reflects the exact locality and step range the agency is actually hiring for.

## A note on non-GS pay systems

Not every federal job uses the GS scale. Some agencies (the Postal Service, parts of Defense, certain excepted-service agencies) use their own pay systems. If a posting doesn't list a GS grade, check its pay section directly rather than assuming GS rules apply.

## The takeaway

The grade tells you the role's level; locality pay tells you what it's actually worth where you live. For remote positions specifically, always confirm which locality table the agency applies — it's the single biggest factor separating the number in your head from the number on your first paycheck.
