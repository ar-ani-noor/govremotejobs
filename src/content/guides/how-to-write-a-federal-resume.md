If you send a typical one-page private-sector resume to a federal job posting, you will very likely be screened out before a human ever evaluates your actual qualifications — not because your experience is lacking, but because federal resumes are evaluated against a different standard entirely.

## Why federal resumes are longer

Federal HR specialists don't just skim for keywords the way many private recruiters do — they compare your resume directly against the qualifications and duties listed in the job announcement, checking whether you can demonstrate the required experience in enough detail to verify it. A resume that says "Managed IT projects" doesn't give a reviewer anything to verify against a required qualification like "experience planning, executing, and closing information technology projects with budgets exceeding $500,000." Federal resumes commonly run 3 to 5 pages because they need to include that level of specific, verifiable detail for every relevant position.

## The details every entry needs

For each job you list, include:

- **Exact dates** — month and year for both start and end, not just years. "2021–2023" isn't sufficient; federal HR needs to calculate exact months of qualifying experience.
- **Hours worked per week** — federal qualification requirements are often stated in terms of months of full-time equivalent experience, so part-time work needs its hours stated for the reviewer to calculate credit correctly.
- **Your supervisor's name and phone number**, and whether they can be contacted — this is standard practice and not providing it can slow down or complicate your application.
- **Series and grade**, if the position was itself a federal job — this tells the reviewer your prior federal experience level directly.
- **Salary**, in some cases — check whether the specific announcement requests it.

## Mirror the announcement's language, deliberately

This isn't about padding your resume with buzzwords — it's about making the reviewer's job of verifying your qualifications as easy as possible. If the announcement's qualifications section says "experience developing and delivering training programs," and your background genuinely includes that, describe it using recognizably similar language, not just a rough paraphrase. A reviewer working through dozens of applications is looking for a clear, direct match, not inference.

## Common mistakes that get resumes screened out

- **Missing dates or hours** — incomplete date information is one of the most common reasons a qualified applicant gets rejected before review, because the system can't calculate whether they meet the minimum months of experience.
- **Copying your LinkedIn summary** — too brief, too vague, no verifiable specifics.
- **Assuming your job title alone proves qualification** — federal reviewers evaluate duties described, not titles held.
- **Applying under the wrong hiring path** — your resume can be excellent and still get rejected if you weren't eligible to apply under that particular announcement's hiring path in the first place.

## A practical approach

Keep a master federal resume with full detail for every position you've held, then tailor the language in each entry's description to match the specific announcement's qualifications section before you submit. It's more work per application than a one-size-fits-all private-sector resume, but it directly determines whether a qualified candidate makes it past the initial screen — which, for federal jobs, is often the hardest part of the entire process.
