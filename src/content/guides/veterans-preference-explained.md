Veterans' preference is one of the more misunderstood parts of federal hiring — it's a real and meaningful advantage, but it works differently than many applicants expect.

## What veterans' preference actually does

Veterans' preference gives eligible veterans additional points added to their numerical score in hiring processes that use a scored ranking, and in some hiring paths, preference-eligible veterans are placed ahead of non-preference candidates in the order agencies consider applicants. It does not guarantee a job, and it does not override the requirement to meet the position's minimum qualifications — you still have to be qualified for the role on the merits.

## Who's eligible

Broadly, veterans' preference applies to:

- Veterans who served during specific wartime periods or in campaigns/expeditions with a campaign badge
- Veterans with a service-connected disability, regardless of when they served (this category, "10-point preference," generally carries stronger preference than standard preference)
- Certain surviving spouses, mothers, and spouses of disabled or deceased veterans, under specific circumstances

The exact eligibility rules have real nuance — active duty alone doesn't automatically qualify you, and the details depend on discharge characterization, service dates, and campaign participation. If you believe you may be eligible, verify your specific category rather than assuming based on general service.

## How to claim it

You claim veterans' preference directly in your USAJOBS application, typically by answering a specific question during the application process and uploading supporting documentation — your DD-214 (Member 4 or Service 2 copy, which shows character of service) and, for disability-based preference, a disability rating letter from the VA.

**Claim it every time you apply, even if you've claimed it before on a different application.** Preference isn't stored on your USAJOBS profile automatically carrying forward to every future application in a way that eliminates the need to document it — check the specific announcement's instructions and make sure your documentation is attached to that application.

## Where it applies (and where it applies less)

Veterans' preference has the most direct, mechanical effect in competitive-service hiring using numerical scoring and "rule of three"-style certificate processes. In excepted-service hiring, direct-hire authorities, and some specialized hiring paths, preference still matters but works somewhat differently — read the specific announcement's hiring path information rather than assuming a uniform process across every posting.

## The realistic summary

Veterans' preference is a genuine, legally protected advantage in federal hiring — but it operates on top of the qualification requirement, not instead of it. The strongest use of preference combines it with a well-built federal resume (see our [federal resume guide](/guides/how-to-write-a-federal-resume)) that clearly demonstrates you meet the announcement's specific qualifications, so the preference points or placement have qualified experience to work with.
