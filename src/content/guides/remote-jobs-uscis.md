US Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) handles the processing of immigration benefits — green cards, naturalization, work authorization, asylum, and related applications — and it's a consistent source of remote and telework-eligible civilian positions, particularly in adjudication, casework, and administrative support roles.

## What USCIS does

USCIS is the agency within the Department of Homeland Security responsible for administering the country's lawful immigration system — reviewing and deciding immigration benefit applications, conducting interviews, and managing the enormous volume of casework that comes with processing millions of applications annually. Much of this work is document review, case analysis, and decision-writing rather than field or enforcement work (which sits with other DHS components, not USCIS).

## Why this produces meaningful remote/telework opportunity

A large share of USCIS's core function — reviewing applications, researching case law and policy, drafting decisions, and managing case files — is desk-based work that doesn't inherently require a specific physical location, particularly as USCIS has expanded electronic case management systems. This makes USCIS a relatively strong source of telework-eligible positions, and it appears consistently among agencies with real remote/telework volume in our data.

## Typical roles

Common position types include: immigration services officers (who review and adjudicate applications), administrative and program support specialists, management and program analysts, IT specialists supporting USCIS's case management systems, and attorneys (see our [government legal jobs guide](/guides/remote-government-legal-jobs)) supporting policy and legal review functions.

## What to check before applying

USCIS positions vary in whether they require specific security clearance or background investigation levels — adjudicative roles handling sensitive immigration and national security information typically require thorough background investigations, which can extend your hiring timeline (see our [application timeline guide](/guides/usajobs-application-timeline)). As always, confirm the specific work location policy — USCIS operates field offices, service centers, and asylum offices across the country, and telework terms depend on which one a specific position is tied to.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do USCIS positions require immigration law experience?** For adjudicative roles like immigration services officers, some level of relevant training is typically provided on the job, and formal immigration law experience isn't always a prerequisite for entry-level positions — check each announcement's specific qualification requirements rather than assuming you need a specialized background to start. Legal and policy-focused roles are more likely to require directly relevant experience.

**Is USCIS work politically sensitive or subject to policy swings?** Immigration policy is a frequently debated area, and adjudication standards and processing priorities can shift with policy changes over time. This doesn't typically affect individual employees' job security in the way it might affect political appointees, but it's worth knowing the mission area can involve more public and political attention than some other federal work.

**How competitive is USCIS hiring?** Like most federal hiring, this varies by role and location, but USCIS processes very high application volumes for many of its positions given the visibility and relative accessibility of immigration services work. A carefully tailored federal resume (see our [guide](/guides/how-to-write-a-federal-resume)) matters more here than in less competitive hiring pools.
